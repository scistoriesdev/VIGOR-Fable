Personal Introduction

As a kid I liked numbers and plants. I was good with numbers and did very well in the Venezuelan math Olympiads, which probably influenced my choice to get a B.S. in Math from my hometown University in Caracas. After graduating, I was very lucky to be able to come to the U.S. to pursue a Ph.D. in Math at Brandeis University. After graduate school, I worked as a software engineer for a few years and eventually joined the human genome project during the time when the genome was being finished, or, as I say “polished” to improve its quality and reduce the regions that still were missing. I fell in love with biology during this time because I realized that all the things I liked doing (math, biology and computer science) could be done together. Twenty years later, with technology always enabling new possibilities, I am still doing the same.

Your Background and Journey

Most of my career has revolved around implementing algorithms for the analysis of sequence data. John (Harris, co-investigator for the VIGOR study) and I began collaborating in 2015 using a device we had built in the laboratory to analyze samples that John’s new approaches to studying vitiligo made possible. Since we began our collaboration, my interests shifted again, and my group is focused to figure out how genetics, combined with the things that we experience every day can put us at risk of having our own immune system turn against us. The VIGOR study is the culmination of four years of work by two graduate students in each of our laboratories. We are certain that pre-clinical symptoms are detectable in skin and also perhaps blood samples prior to the appearance of any vitiligo symptoms. The questions we seek to answer are: how early do these symptoms arise? And what triggers the conditions that lead to vitiligo? Once found, the answers will help us (and others) develop preventive measures that can reduce the incidence of vitiligo and other skin conditions.

Outside of Work

Besides spending time with my wife Karen, our two cats and our two children (whenever they come to visit), I spend a lot of time gardening. I have always liked plants and found orchids to be particularly fascinating. As a teenager, I would go to construction sites to save the orchids that grew on the trees that had been cleared from the site. At one point I had hundreds of plants growing on a small four foot by seven foot balcony in our 8th story apartment in Caracas. Here are a couple orchids in bloom at home in Venezuela - both are more than 40 years old. I still grow some here in New England, but it is a much more challenging endeavor. I don’t have as many and find the fight against the cold and dryness of the northeast very challenging, but once the orchid bug stings, it never leaves.
