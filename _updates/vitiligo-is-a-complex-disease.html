The Vitiligo Impact of family Groups: Observation and Research (“VIGOR”) study is attempting to answer an important question: what causes vitiligo? But why is this question so hard to answer and why is it important to do so? The “why” is more straightforward – knowing what causes vitiligo will help physicians to one day predict who will get it and then prevent this condition. But what about the more complicated first question? What makes understanding the causes of vitiligo so challenging?

Genes + Environment = Disease

We know that genetics play an important role vitiligo. An individual’s risk of developing vitiligo is seven times higher if they have a first-degree relative, i.e. a sibling or parent, with the condition. Modern genetic approaches have allowed researchers (including members of our research team!) to identify variants of several genes that are associated with vitiligo. While previous genetic studies have helped our understanding of risk factors for disease, it can’t explain the entire risk. Consider the case of identical twins. Identical twins share exactly the same genetic information. Therefore, you might imagine that if one twin developed vitiligo then the other one would be certain to also develop vitiligo. However, this is not the case. Only about 1 in 4 pairs of identical twins will both siblings develop vitiligo. While having an identical twin with vitiligo represents the highest level of risk for this condition, it clearly shows that something beyond genetics is at play.

So, if vitiligo risk isn’t entirely genetics, then what is it? Broadly speaking it is what we call “the environment” but we mean this term to represent all of the external factors that a person encounters in their lifetime. A wide range of environmental factors have been associated with vitiligo, including: viral infections, chemicals such as those found in hair dyes, stress, and the microbiome (the normal bacteria that live on and in your body). Making the situation more complicated is that vitiligo might not result from a single environmental exposure, but instead from the way that particular combinations of environmental exposures interact with the unique genetic makeup of an individual!

A VIGOR-ous attempt to understand a complex situation

The VIGOR study is enrolling patients with vitiligo and their first-degree relatives without vitiligo. As we mentioned earlier, having a close relative with vitiligo makes it much more likely that a person will develop vitiligo. If we can identify and follow people more likely to develop vitiligo, we have the opportunity to look for clues in their genetics, features of their skin, and environmental exposures to determine what triggers the condition. For our study to accomplish this goal, we need to recruit many more first-degree relatives without vitiligo than people with vitiligo because most of these relatives won’t develop vitiligo, even despite their increased risk. Because we don’t know when vitiligo might appear in an at-risk person, we’ll need to follow everyone for years. However, when an at-risk person in our study develops vitiligo, we will have information about them that has never been possible to obtain before. This gives us an unprecedented ability to better understand what causes vitiligo. There is no question that it is ambitious project, but we are excited for the challenge and can’t wait to give you updates as we make progress.
