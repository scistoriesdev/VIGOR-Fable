Imagine two separate kingdoms, both are large in size and full of valuable resources. As you can imagine, both kingdoms are highly sought after and thus are under constant attack by foreign invaders. To protect the themselves, each kingdom has built up an army of soldiers whose job it is to defend its borders and keep their people safe.

In one kingdom, the army is moderately strong, and under strict control by their ruler. These soldiers have very little autonomy because of many checks and balances that control their actions. This army does a relatively good job at identifying and controlling invaders, although occasionally the kingdom’s people experience losses because the invaders get through and because of the checks and balances, it takes time to control them.

In the other kingdom, the army is stronger and has been granted more autonomy. Here, invaders are quickly identified and destroyed, and the kingdom is better protected against enemy invasion. However, because of its autonomy, this army occasionally misidentifies residents of the kingdom as invaders and destroys them, causing damage to the kingdom and its people by its own hand.

In this story, the kingdoms are the human body, and the armies are the immune system. The invaders are infectious agents such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites, or cancerous cells, all of which need to be destroyed by immune cells. The residents of the kingdoms are your own tissues and organs, which can also be damaged by the immune system if it is overzealous. The character of the immune system is influenced by your genes and previous encounters with infectious agents or cancer. If a person has a slightly weaker and highly controlled immune system, infections and cancer can be more common. However, if the immune system is stronger and/or has more autonomy, autoimmunity, or damage to your own tissues caused by your immune system, can occur. Thankfully, in most cases the immune system is balanced, so infections and cancer are adequately controlled but autoimmunity does not occur.

Examples of common autoimmune diseases include type 1 diabetes, where the insulin-making cells in the pancreas are destroyed, and vitiligo, where the pigment-making cells in the skin are destroyed. There are many other examples of autoimmunity, including diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, and lupus.

Once an autoimmune disease has been diagnosed, there are various treatments available to help manage symptoms and prevent further damage to the body. A first goal of treatment is to restore or compensate for the damage inflicted on the damaged tissue. For example, type 1 diabetics must take insulin to make up for their body’s inability to make it. The second goal is to control the inappropriate immune response so that no further damage is inflicted on the body. For many years and still today, a first line of treatment to control the inappropriate immune response is to suppress the immune system with systemic medications such as steroids. However this type of treatment weakens all aspects of the immune system, making it more likely that the individual will contract infections or develop cancer. Researchers are working hard to develop better treatments that are more “targeted”, or specific to the dysfunction in the immune system that caused autoimmunity so that the rest of the immune system remains intact to fight infections and cancer. An example of this is available for individuals with vitiligo. In 2022, the FDA approved a topical cream (Opzelura) that inhibits the immune system’s response in the skin, allowing melanocytes to survive and repigment the vitiligo lesions. Since the treatment is only in the affected parts of the skin, the rest of the immune system remains able to function normally.

The future of fighting autoimmune diseases will be to develop approaches to predict and prevent their onset in the first place. The new VIGOR study at UMass Chan Medical School is a first step towards this ultimate goal.

While the decreased incidence of melanoma in vitiligo patients highlights the idea that a more autoimmune-prone immune system is better able to fight off invaders, do we know if that is generally true?
