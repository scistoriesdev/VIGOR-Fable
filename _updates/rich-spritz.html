Personal Introduction

I am now Emeritus Professor of Pediatrics at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, where I was previously Director of the Human Medical Genetics and Genomics Program. I began my career in Human Genetics long before anyone had any idea of a "Human Genome Project" or indeed before there was even the word "Genomics". In fact, I had a primary role in the first direct analysis of any human gene, and discovered the first human gene mutation, in a blood disease called "thalassemia". This opened the door to what eventually became the Human Genome Project and the practice of Molecular Medicine.

Your Background and Journey

I am happy to say I am now retired. But more than 50 years ago, I first became engaged in the field of genetics while an undergraduate at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, studying with James Crow. After that, I studied Medicine at Pennsylvania State University, where in parallel I carried out research in cancer virology and biology. After receiving my M.D. degree, I served as intern and resident in Pediatrics at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, but at the same time carried out genetic research with Bill Mellman at the University of Pennsylvania, successfully mapping a specific gene, GOT1, to a human chromosome. Next, and most importantly, I was a Fellow in Human Genetics at Yale University, where again simultaneous with clinical training, I worked with Sherman Weissman and Bernie Forget to isolate and sequence the first human genes (for hemoglobin), and identifying the very first human gene mutation (in thalassemia). Subsequently, as a Professor at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, my laboratory group went on to identify and sequence genes underlying many different pigmentation diseases, including albinism, piebaldism, Waardenburg syndrome, Chediak-Higashi syndrome, Hermansky-Pudlak syndrome, and many others, and in parallel we identified genes for several other diseases, most importantly cleft lip and palate. On moving to the University of Colorado, we switched gears to work on vitiligo. Over 25 years, carrying out studies that included more than 8000 vitiligo patients and their relatives from around the world, we ultimately identified 50 different genes that contribute to vitiligo, painting a much clearer picture of the underpinnings of vitiligo genetic risk, the underlying biology, and vitiligo epidemiology. To date, our research has resulted in almost 300 papers published in the medical literature.

Outside of Work

I have always had an adventurous streak. When I was younger I was an accomplished mountain climber, with ascents of difficult rock walls and peaks around the world. Today, in winter I am a ski instructor at Breckenridge ski resort in Colorado, in summer am an international race car driver, and year-round am a concert cellist. In early 2024 I will take part in a series of races in New Zealand.
